    The problem

    Before we fly, we rehearse on Earth

    Every rover wheel, drill and spacesuit that reaches the Moon or Mars has first been tested somewhere on Earth: in deserts, on glaciers, in volcanic fields, inside impact craters and caves. These places are called analog sites.

    Most known analogs were chosen by expert judgement, and many are only described in general terms. NASA's challenge asks a sharper question: can Earth, Moon and Mars data find new analogs systematically, and explain exactly where each comparison holds and where it breaks?

    Rehearsing on Earth

    Underwater, in the Arctic, in the desert

    NASA crews and scientists have trained in analog environments since the Apollo era: astronauts learned geology in volcanic fields, aquanauts practice spacewalks on the sea floor, and rovers are driven across deserts before they are trusted on another world.

    How it works

    Three steps from a crater on Mars to a valley on Earth

    "Which place on Earth looks like this crater?" is usually answered by experience and photographs. Orbitrary turns it into a comparison you can check: the same eight measurements on both worlds, a score for each, and an honest note on what is missing.

    1. 1

      Pick a target

      Choose the Moon or Mars, one of four missions, and a site: a known landing zone or base candidate from our list, or any point you click on the globe in Explore mode.

    2. 2

      Compare in 8 categories

      The target is compared with Earth regions category by category. The mission decides which categories weigh more, and missing data is marked as missing instead of being treated as zero.

    3. 3

      See the top 3 analogs

      Each result comes with a map, an eight-axis profile, a plain-language explanation of what matches and what doesn't, its data coverage, and warnings.

    Missions

    Four missions, four reasons to rehearse

    An analog is only "good" for a purpose. A flat, dusty plain is ideal for testing a rover and nearly useless for searching for traces of life. So before comparing places, you choose what you want to rehearse, and each mission puts more weight on the categories that matter for it.

    Mobility test

    Can a rover or a crew move safely here?

    Driving, navigation and traction on loose soil, climbing slopes and steering around boulders. Mobility analogs are where wheels, autonomous navigation software and spacewalk routes are tested before they are trusted on another world.

    Key categoriesTopographyTopologyDust

    Geology

    Can we learn to read the rocks here?

    Field geology training, choosing which samples to collect, and testing instruments on rocks that formed the way lunar or Martian rocks did: by volcanism, impacts, water or ice. Astronauts train their eyes on Earth so they recognise what matters on the surface.

    Key categoriesGeologyMineralogyTopology

    Ice & resources

    Can a base live off the land here?

    Prospecting for water ice and water-bearing minerals, and testing drills and extraction methods. Using local resources, known as in-situ resource utilization, is key for a permanent base: water means drinking water, oxygen and rocket fuel.

    Key categoriesWater/IceThermalIllumination

    Astrobiology

    Could life have existed, and how would we recognise its traces?

    Extreme environments on Earth, from hyper-arid deserts to salt flats and cold springs, show where microbes survive and which minerals preserve biosignatures. They help decide where a rover should search and what its instruments must be able to detect.

    Key categoriesWater/IceMineralogyThermal

    How we chose these four

    FROM THE CHALLENGE

    NASA's challenge lists the dimensions an analog can share: geology, terrain, temperature, water and ice, dust, biology, subsurface and conditions for robotic or human operations.

    GROUPED BY PURPOSE

    We grouped these dimensions by what a team would actually rehearse on Earth: moving across the surface, reading its rocks, finding resources, and looking for signs of life.

    DIFFERENT WEIGHTS

    All four missions use the same eight categories, but weigh them differently. The exact weights will be published with the method.

    Under the hood

    Method, topology and validation

    WHAT MAKES ORBITRARY DIFFERENT

    We read the shape of a landscape, not only its numbers.

    Two craters can have the same height, the same slope and the same roughness, and still look nothing alike. What tells them apart is how their pits, rims and ridges are arranged. Orbitrary measures that arrangement with topological data analysis and compares it between the Moon or Mars and every Earth candidate.

    Classic comparison
    How high? How steep?Averages of elevation and slope. Blind to layout.
    Orbitrary adds
    How is it shaped?Pits, rims and ridges as a fingerprint, independent of absolute height.
    1 · Flood the terrainWater rises slowly over each 20 × 20 km window; every pit becomes a lake.
    2 · Record births and deathsEach lake appears at one height and disappears when it merges with an older one: one point on a persistence diagram.
    3 · Compare fingerprintsThe diagram is the window's shape fingerprint. Target and Earth fingerprints are compared directly.

    Computed for the 30 best Earth windows of every target and added to their score as one of the eight categories.

    Live: a slice through a terrain. As the water rises, each lake is born and dies when it merges into an older lake; its (birth, death) pair appears on the persistence diagram on the right.

    METHOD

    How the score is built

    Per-category similarities combined with mission weights into one absolute score.

    P = Gate · Σ w·s / Σ w
    s = similarity in one category (share of reference Earth windows that are further from the target); w = mission weight; the sum runs over categories with data on both sides.

    Limits

    What Earth can't imitate

    Some conditions have no analog on our planet. Every result should be read with these in mind.

    • Gravity. The Moon has about 1/6 and Mars about 3/8 of Earth's gravity. Dust, rocks and wheels behave differently.
    • Vacuum. The Moon has essentially no atmosphere, so there is no wind, no sound and no protection from micrometeorites.
    • Thin atmosphere. Mars's surface pressure is under 1% of Earth's, too thin for liquid water to stay stable at most places.
    • Radiation. Without a global magnetic field and a thick atmosphere, surface radiation is far higher than anywhere on Earth.
